\chapter{Background}

Probabilistic programming is a paradigm where we are defining statistical models using code. It is a means of describing
complex distributions over some space in a structured way. Probabilistic programming languages (PPLs) also come with an interpreter
(probabilistic inference engine) which allows the programmer to sample a term from the distribution denoted by a program
and indeed sample distributions while constructing new distributions.
 More interestingly, Bayesian probabilistic programming languages (Bppl) also allow one to ``observe'' some data and update
their belief of the world (update the probability distribution they associate with some occurrence). Lilac does not support Bayesian
updates, so we will not be seeing much of this important feature of probabilistic programming.

In order to understand the semantics of probabilistic programming we first need a crash course on measure theory, and then explore a compositional
semantics for PPLs. We turn our attention to the theory of separation logics abstracting a prototypical instance, till we reach the core ideas,
on top of which Lilac will be built in Chapter \ref{sec:definitions}. Finally, we seek a fundamental understanding of our premise of foundational
interactive verification: the foundations in dependent type theory, and the meta-programming which makes it ``interactive''.

\section{Measure Theory}
\label{sec:measure-theory}
In order to understand the semantics of a probabilistic programming, a formal definition for probability is needed.
Measure theory underpins probability theory and an overview is given here. We want to be able to talk about the
probabilities of some event occurring, which is modelled by a \texttt{random variable}. In order to define a random
variable we first need to define measures and measurable spaces. A measurable space is a pair $(\Omega, \Sigma_\Omega)$,
where $\Omega$ is of type \texttt{Set}, and $\Sigma_\Omega \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\Omega)$ is called the $\sigma$-algebra. We interpret each element of $\Sigma_\Omega$
to be an event (such as a coin flip landing heads). We would like the $\sigma$-algebra to be closed under complements and
countable unions\footnote{from unions and complements we obtain intersections through de Morgan's law}. From the presentation so far, it may seem like all singleton sets must be events but
due to technical reasons (\citep[Chapter 2]{axler}), this cannot be in certain cases where $\Sigma$ is infinite.

Formally, a $\sigma$-algebra $\Sigma_\Omega \subseteq \mathcal{P}(\Omega)$ is a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ satisfying:
\begin{align*}
  &\Omega \in \Sigma_\Omega, \\
  &E \in \Sigma_\Omega \implies \Omega \setminus E \in \Sigma_\Omega, \\
  &(E_i)_{i \in \mathbb{N}} \in \Sigma_\Omega \implies \bigcup_{i \in \mathbb{N}} E_i \in \Sigma_\Omega.
\end{align*}

A measure on a measurable space is a function
 $\mu : \Sigma_\Omega \rightarrow [0, \infty]$. 

A probability measure on the other hand is more restrictive: $\mu : \Sigma_\Omega \rightarrow [0, 1]$.
It gives the probability of an event $E \in \Sigma\_\Omega$ occurring.
A similar but more general notion than what a measure gives us, is a random variable: a measurable
function between two measurable spaces. We say "more general notion", since using the identity measurable function
yields the measure we started with. A measurable function denoted $f : (\Omega_1, \Sigma_\Omega)  \marrow (\Omega_2, \G)$
$f : \Omega_1  \marrow \Omega_2$ is well-defined when there is an ambient measurable space structure $(\Omega_1, \Sigma_\Omega)$
and $(\Omega_2, \G)$. $f$ allows us to talk about the distribution of a particular attribute of in the event space. For example
$\Omega_1 = \{1,\ldots,6\}^2$ may be the set of all events associated with playing 2 dice, while $f$ may map to the sum of the two dice, $\Omega_2 = \{1,\ldots,12\}$.
A measurable function $f$, transforms any measure $\mu$ on $(\Omega_1, \Sigma_\Omega)$ to the pushforward measure $\mu'$ on $(\Omega_2, \G)$.
In order for this to be well-defined we require the following measurability condition to be satisfied by all measurable functions:
$\forall G \in \G, f^{-1}(G) \in \Sigma_\Omega$.
We denote the pushforward as $\mu' = f_\#\mu$.

A related notion to the pushforward measure, is the pullback $\sigma$-algebra. Instead of constraining the measurable function to behave according to the $\sigma$-algebra
on the domain, the pullback asks what $\sigma$-algebra do we require on the domain to support some measurable function. Given a function $f : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega'$
and a $\sigma$-algebra $\G$ on the codomain $\Omega'$, the pullback $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$ is
\[
f^{*}\G := \{\, f^{-1}(G) \mid G \in \G \,\}.
\]
This is the coarsest (smallest) $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$ making $f$ measurable, and we are guaranteed is closed under complements and
countable unions since $\G$ is a $sigma$-algebra. 

A Lebesgue integral is a generalisation of the standard (Riemann) integral. Intuitively the Lebesgue and Riemann integral will coincide on the spaces such as $\mathbb{R}$,
where the domain of integration is continuous. The Lebesgue integral relaxes that requirement. The Lebesgue integral of a measurable function $f : \Omega \rightarrow [0, \infty]$
with respect to a measure $\mu : \Sigma_\Omega \rightarrow [0, \infty] $ is a number defined by:

\[
\int_{\Omega} f\,d\mu := \sup \left\{
\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(\omega_i)\,\mu(U_i)
\;\middle|\;
\{U_i \in \Sigma_\Omega\}_{i=1}^{n} \text{ disjoint cover of } \Omega
\right\}.
\]

To get an intuition remember that integrals are supposed to add the values of function by breaking the domain up into infinitesimal pieces.
Observe that the integral is a weighted sum of $f(\omega)$, weighted by the measure. The integral is iterating over the entire space
by picking some choice of measurable disjoint sets whose union is the $\Omega$ (disjoint cover of $\Omega$). And for each measurable set ($U_i$) it picks a representative
point $\omega$ evaluates f on $\omega$ and weights it by how large the set is ($\mu U_i)$. The supremum and infimum together enforce that the chosen disjoint
cover is chosen as finely (small measure) as possible.

\subsection{Borel spaces and Lebesgue measures}
Intuitively a measure is supposed to be a length, volume or in general a ``size'' in a higher dimensional counterpart. 
The standard borel $\sigma$-algebra on $\mathbb{R}$, $B_\mathbb{R}$, corresponds to our notion of those subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ which have a ``size''. Again we refer
to~\citet[Chapter 2]{axler}, for examples of subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ which are not measurable sets, which are rather obscure. In general
most subsets that we care about: open and closed interval, singletons and of course unions and complements of the previous, are all included in $B_\mathbb{R}$.
We define the $\sigma$-algebra, \emph{generated} out of some $S : Set (Set A)$, $\sigma(S)$ as the smallest set containing A and is closed under complements
and countable unions. We can see what $B_\mathbb{R}$ includes by seeing various ways of defining it:

We have that the Borel $\sigma$-algebra $B_\mathbb{R} = \sigma(S)$, when $S$ is:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(a)] $\{(a, b) : a, b \in \mathbb{R}\}$,
  \item[(b)] $\{(-\infty, a) : a \in \mathbb{R}\}$,
  \item[(c)] $\{(-\infty, a] : a \in \mathbb{R}\}$,
  \item[(d)] $\{(a, \infty) : a \in \mathbb{R}\}$.
\end{enumerate}

More generally a Borel $\sigma$-algbera, (not necessarily on $\mathbb{R}$), is a $\sigma$ algebra generated from the topology and the standard Borel
$\sigma$-algebra is canonical since it is generated from the standard topology~\citep{axler}. We are only concerned with Borel $\sigma$-algebras for $\mathbb{R}^n$ (i.e. Euclidean space) in
this project.

Returning to the initial claim that a measure is intuitively the size of a set, we present the Lebesgue measure on $\mathbb{R}, B_\mathbb{R}$, $\lambda$, which corresponds to the sums of lengths of all
intervals in a measurable subset. Precisely:
\[
\lambda(E) := \inf \left\{
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} (b_i - a_i)
\;\middle|\;
E \subseteq \bigcup_{i=1}^{\infty} (a_i, b_i)
\right\}.
\]

That is, the Lebesgue measure of a set $E$ is the infimum, over all countable covers of $E$ by open intervals, of the
total length of those intervals. On an interval it recovers the expected value, $\lambda\big((a, b)\big) = b - a$, and it
assigns measure zero to every singleton (and hence to every countable set, such as $\mathbb{Q}$).

\subsection{Product of measurable spaces $\times_m$ and product measures $\otimes$}
In the previous section we mentioned that we are interested in measurable spaces (and measures) over $\mathbb{R}^n$ for some n. Let's see how we can build the
standard Borel $\sigma$-algebra and Lebesgue measure over $\mathbb{R}^n$ by taking products.

Given two measurable spaces $(\Omega_1, \Sigma_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, \Sigma_2)$, their product is the measurable space on the Cartesian
product $\Omega_1 \times \Omega_2$, whose $\sigma$-algebra is generated by the \emph{measurable rectangles}:
\[
\Sigma_1 \otimes \Sigma_2 := \sigma\big(\{\, A \times B \mid A \in \Sigma_1,\ B \in \Sigma_2 \,\}\big).
\]
Note we must take the generated $\sigma$-algebra: the rectangles themselves are not closed under complement or countable union. We
write the resulting measurable space as $(\Omega_1, \Sigma_1) \times_m (\Omega_2, \Sigma_2)$.

Given $\sigma$-finite measures $\mu_1$ on $\Sigma_1$ and $\mu_2$ on $\Sigma_2$, there is a unique \emph{product measure}
$\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2$ on $\Sigma_1 \otimes \Sigma_2$ determined on rectangles by
\[
(\mu_1 \otimes \mu_2)(A \times B) = \mu_1(A)\,\mu_2(B),
\]
so the measure of a rectangle is the product of the side measures (for uniqueness see \citet[Chapter 5A]{axler}). Specialising to $\mathbb{R}$, the $n$-fold product of $(\mathbb{R}, B_\mathbb{R})$ with itself gives the standard Borel
$\sigma$-algebra $B_{\mathbb{R}^n} = B_\mathbb{R}^{\otimes n}$, and the $n$-fold product of the Lebesgue measure gives the
$n$-dimensional Lebesgue measure, $\lambda_n = \lambda^{\otimes n}$, assigning to a \emph{measurable rectangle} $\prod_{i=1}^n (a_i, b_i)$ its size
$\prod_{i=1}^n (b_i - a_i)$ as one would expect.

\subsection{Probability Theory}
\label{sec:indep}
Probability theory emerges from measure theory, and is centred around the probability space which is a tuple $(\Omega, \Sigma_\Omega, \mu)$, of a set equipped with a measurable space,
and a probability measure. Random variables play a key role as well so we provide a little more intuition on their relation to the measurable space in their (co)domain. 
To bolster the intuition that the measurability condition is less restrictive over a finer measurable space in the domain, imagine a coarse measurable space in the domain. The pre-image
of the function is less likely to be a measurable set in the domain, and so less likely to satisfy the measurability condition. Hence coarser measurable spaces in the domain are more restrictive.
Take the premise that we want to be as permissive as possible in the random variables we are allowed to define. Then we want their domain to be as general as possible, so we take a measurable space which is very 
fine grained; in other words it is very large as it
contains a large number of sets. In practice this is often achieved by equipping the domain with the standard Borel measurable space, though this is not necessarily the finest (greatest) measurable space available
(indeed, a greatest measurable space does not exist for general $\Omega$).

Two random variables $X : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega_1$ and $Y : \Omega \rightarrow \Omega_2$ on a probability space $(\Omega, \Sigma_\Omega, \mu)$ are
\emph{independent} when knowing the value of one tells us nothing about the other, that is, their joint distribution factors into the
product of their distributions:
\[
\mu\big(X^{-1}(A) \cap Y^{-1}(B)\big) = \mu\big(X^{-1}(A)\big)\,\mu\big(Y^{-1}(B)\big) \qquad \forall A \in \Sigma_1,\ B \in \Sigma_2.
\]

Finally, the \emph{expectation} of a real-valued random variable $X : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is its average value weighted by the
probability measure, given by the Lebesgue integral
\[
\mathbb{E}[X] := \int_{\Omega} X \,d\mu.
\]

\section{Semantics of Probabilistic Programs}
\label{sec:appl}

We first present Appl \citep[appendix A]{lilac}, a probabilistic programming language without Bayesian updates. Syntactically it looks like a basic WHILE language.
In Appl, there isn't an explicit \texttt{sample} construct. Sampling is realised in the semantics of sequencing as explained later. Though the language is
first order, PPLs still have a non-trivial, novel denotational semantics. The central difference is that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Types are interpreted in \texttt{Meas}, the category of measurable spaces rather than
just \texttt{Set}. Concretely each type is interpreted as a measurable space $(A, \Sigma_A)$.
\item Probabilistic programs have a monadic semantics, interpreted in the Giry monad, which expresses probabilistic computation as an effect. The Giry monad is a monad in \texttt{Meas}.
\end{enumerate}
The interesting aspects of Appl, follow from these two points which we will explain thoroughly.

A note on notation: we deliberately overload a single letter, writing $A$ both for a syntactic type of Appl and for its denotation $\sem{A}$, the measurable space it is interpreted as. Which is meant is always clear from context, since the syntactic type constructor is written $\mathbf{G}$ while its semantic counterpart, the Giry functor, is written $\G$.

and clearly the greatest one (ordered by subset inclusion). In our PPL we would like to express
measures and manipulate measures.

\subsection{The Giry monad}
Our intuition is that syntactic \texttt{ret} and let-binding should have something to do with the Giry monad's operations. denoted as:

\[
\begin{array}{c@{\qquad\qquad}c}
\mathrm{ret}_\G : A \to \G A  & \bind : \G A \to (A \to \G B) \to \G B
\end{array}
\]


Here we are assuming $A$ and $B$ have the form $A = \sem{A}$ and $B = \sem{B}$
Let us see how we can construct these operations and validate the monad laws. We define $\mathrm{ret}_\G\, x = \mathrm{dirac}\, x$. The Dirac measure concentrates all its probability at a single
point. Given a measurable set $E$, $\mathrm{dirac} x E = \text{if } x \in E \text{ then } 1 \text{ else } 0$.

Now the type $(A \to \G A)$ is defined as $\text{Kernel } A\, B$. Mathlib uses this type as the primitive on which most of its probability theory library is built.
We explore a diagrammatic exploration of probabilistic programs to see why kernels are so compelling:

For a measurable space $(A, \Sigma_A)$, a probability measure is of type
$\mu : \Sigma_A \rightarrow [0, 1]$. This gives a distribution on A. The intuition is that given $\mu$ we can sample some value from it.
Each triangle below is a kernel. And the types of its input and
output are indicated. We compose the kernels along the type B as $\kappa \underset{B}{\circ} \iota$. The types of the individual kernels and composed kernel of Fig \ref{fig:semantics},
is given below:

\begin{align}

\kappa &: B \to \G C \\
\kappa \underset{B}{\circ} \iota &: A \to \G C
\end{align}

The interpretation of the kernel $\iota$ is that for every (deterministic) value of $A$ it gets, it will give you a (probabilistic value) distribution over type $B$.
So composition here, is sampling from the $\G B$, getting a deterministic value of type $B$ and passing that to the kernel $\kappa$. This sampling
is done ranging over all the values of $B$ in proportion to their distribution, and taking a weighted average of the values of type $\G C$. More precisely,
this is the integral: $\kappa \underset{B}{\circ} \iota = \lambda a. \int \lambda b. \; \kappa(b) \; d\iota(a)$ 

Notice that $\circ$ has a type very similar to the $\bind$ (in the Giry monad).
% We now write $\G(A)$ instead of \texttt{Measure} $A$, for the Giry functor. 
$\circ : (B \to \G C) \to (A \to \G B) \to \G C$. If we just swap the arguments and partially apply an $a : A$ on $\iota$, we see
that the bind of the Giry monad composes a measure with a kernel like so $\iota(a) \bind \kappa : \G C$
We now take a step back, and have a look at a more general problem.

Having gained some intuition for how $\bind$ works let's provide its definition. In Lean, it is defined as follows in Mathlib:

\begin{leancode}
def bind (μ : Measure α) (κ : α → Measure β) : Measure β :=
  join (map κ μ)
\end{leancode}

\texttt{map} refers to taking a pushforward, so $\mathrm{map}\, \mu\, \kappa = \kappa_\#\mu : \G (\G \beta)$. This makes sense; for each $a : \alpha$ we get some $\G \beta$ following $\kappa$, so the measure on $\alpha$
naturally turns into a measure on measures on $\beta$. \texttt{join}\footnote{\texttt{bind} is derived from join in a usual categorical presentation of a monad} collapses the type: $\mathrm{join} : \G (\G \beta) \to \G \beta$.
It takes some measurable set $E : \Sigma_\beta$ and the measure it
returns is the weighted sum of all the measures in $\kappa_\#\mu : \G (\G \beta)$. Precisely it is the integral

$\mathrm{join}\, \mu'\, E = \int (\lambda\, \mu \mapsto \mu(E))\ d\mu'$

Unfolding the definitions we have:
$(\mu_\alpha \bind \kappa)\, E = \int (\lambda\, \mu_\beta \mapsto \mu_\beta(E))\ d(\kappa_\#\mu_\alpha)$.

We prove one of the monad laws to check that these definitions make sense. Let us prove that $\mathrm{join}\, (\mathrm{ret}\, \mu) = \mu$. Take an arbitrary measurable set $E$, and we prove through
extensionality:

$\mathrm{join}\, (\mathrm{ret}\, \mu) E = \int (\lambda\, \mu' \mapsto \mu'(E))\ d(\mathrm{dirac}\ \mu)$

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{join}\, (\mathrm{ret}\, \mu) E \\ 
&= \int (\lambda \mu' \mapsto \mu'(E))\ d(\mathrm{dirac}\, \mu) \\
&= (\lambda \mu' \mapsto \mu'(E))\ \mu \\
&= \mu(E)
\end{aligned}
\]

As expected. We justify the step dropping the integral, with the fact that $\mathrm{dirac} \mu$ concentrates all of its probability on $\mu$. Viewing the integral as a weighted sum, a weight of 1 is given
to $\mu$. Next we take a step back from Appl and consider a wider setting.

Though we presented the kernel as a single type, there is in fact a taxonomy of different types of kernels, based on the restrictions on the measure of their output type. In Appl, we are

 $\kappa : A \to \G(B)$ is finite if there is finite $r \in [0, \infty)$ such that, for all a, $\kappa(a, B) < r$.
$\kappa$ is s-finite if there is a sequence $\kappa_1 \; \ldots \; \kappa_n \; \ldots$ of finite
kernels and $\Sigma_{i=1\ldots \infty} \; \kappa_i = \kappa$.

so it is useful to know about them even if in this project we are only concerned with Markov kernels.
